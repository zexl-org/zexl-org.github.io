Today's Brief

Inflation is cooling in America and refusing to in Japan. The same barrel of oil is doing both.

Read the case behind each question

Four questions above, four short pieces below. Each explains why two sets of informed people are looking at the same facts and reaching different answers — and what would settle it.

01 · Prices — the Fed in September

Two cooling prints in two days, and the committee is exactly as split as it was.

Consumer prices came in at 3.4% yesterday, core at 2.5%, both a tenth below June and both on consensus. Market odds for a September rise eased another three points to around 51%. Published economist forecasts have not moved once all week.

What has not changed is the argument. Beth Hammack, one of three who dissented for an immediate rise on 29 July, wrote publicly on Tuesday that now is the time to act. Cooler data does not answer her case, which was never about the current reading — it was about how expensive the return to 2% becomes if you wait.

After this morning's producer figure, the August data cycle is effectively closed. Nothing new arrives until payrolls on 4 September and August CPI on 11 September, five days before the decision. Three weeks of silence, and a fifty-point gap between the two groups who claim to know.

WatchWhether Kashkari or Logan follows Hammack into public argument during the quiet weeks. Three dissenters speaking is a different signal from one.

02 · Prices — the factory gate, in one hour

June's fall was one commodity. That commodity has since risen four sessions.

Producer prices fell 0.3% in June, and the reason is legible in a single line of the release: diesel dropped 18% in the month. Gasoline, jet fuel and crude petroleum fell with it. Final demand goods were down 1.4% while services rose 0.2%.

The annual rate at 5.5% still sits two points above what consumers pay. Forecasters expect 4.9% this morning, which would narrow the gap. But the arithmetic that produced June's decline does not repeat: crude rose four straight sessions into this week, and stage-one intermediate demand was still running at 11.0% year-on-year in June even after the energy collapse.

What makes this the last useful reading is timing. It is the final inflation input before the committee's quiet period, and the one that would show a pass-through if a pass-through exists.

WatchFinal demand services rather than goods. Goods track oil directly; services are where a genuine pass-through would appear first.

Sources: BLS Producer Price Index, June 2026 · economist estimates for July

03 · Prices — Japan, and the other direction

Japanese firms paid 7.2% more than a year ago. American consumers paid 3.4%.

The Bank of Japan reported this morning that corporate goods prices rose 7.2% year-on-year in July, barely below June's upwardly revised 7.3% — the highest since March 2023. Monthly, they rose 0.1%. Oil and coal products, chemicals and non-ferrous metals led the advance.

The mechanism is the reason this belongs next to the American questions. Japan imports almost all of its energy and pays for it in dollars. The same crude that is cooling US inflation through a firm dollar and domestic supply is arriving in Tokyo at a currency-adjusted premium, and it lands on producers before anyone else.

The Board raised to 1.0% in June, the first time at that level in three decades, on a 7–1 vote, and has said it will keep going while watching how the Middle East situation feeds through. A 7.2% input cost is not a number that argues for stopping. Japan's next policy decision follows this data by weeks, and a move there sets the yen, which sets the price of everything the rest of the world buys from Japan.

WatchWhether the monthly rate stays near 0.1% or reaccelerates. The annual figure is still carrying last year's spike; the monthly one shows what is happening now.

Sources: Bank of Japan, Corporate Goods Price Index, July 2026 preliminary · BOJ policy statement, 16 Jun 2026

04 · Markets — the calm

Volatility drained out of a week that produced two inflation prints and no answers.

The VIX fell nearly 5% today to 14.6, its calmest since before July's oil spike. The S&P sits at 7,748, the Nasdaq at 26,588 and up half a percent, the dollar index firm at 99.9. WTI eased back below $83.

On its own that is a market deciding the inflation question is settled. What it sits next to is a fifty-point disagreement about the September meeting, a producer price release in under an hour, and an economy where wages have trailed prices for four consecutive months.

The asymmetry is the point. The index cannot fall much below 12 without signalling outright complacency, but it reached 31 in March in a matter of days when crude first spiked. Calm markets are usually right, which is why they are calm. When they are wrong it happens in a single session.

WatchWhether equity and Treasury volatility diverge. Persistent gaps between the two have preceded regime shifts more reliably than either alone.

Sources: Cboe VIX and index levels, 12–13 Aug 2026
