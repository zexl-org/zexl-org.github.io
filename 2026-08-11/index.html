Read the case behind each question

Four questions above, four short pieces below. Each explains why two sets of informed people are looking at the same facts and reaching different answers — and what would settle it.

01 · Prices — the Fed in September

Eighteen points in three sessions, and not one of them came from economic data.

Last Wednesday the market put September odds at 44%. Friday's payroll report — a fall of 23,000 against a forecast of +83,000 — should have pushed that lower still. Instead it now sits near 62%, and the whole move traces to a single input: Brent has risen four sessions in a row.

That is the awkward part. A central bank facing weak employment cuts rates; a central bank facing an energy shock raises them. This one is facing both inside the same week, and the market has decided which half it thinks the Committee will weight.

Published economist forecasts have not moved at all. They still assume no rise this year, on the view that oil unwinds before September. Neither side has new data — they have the same data and different weights.

WatchWhether any of the three July dissenters — Hammack, Kashkari or Logan — speaks publicly before the meeting. Under this chair the vote split is the clearest guidance available.

02 · Prices — tomorrow's inflation print

Everyone expects 2.5%. June expected 2.9% and delivered 2.6%.

Reuters polling puts core inflation at 2.5% year-on-year for July, down a tenth from June. Headline is seen at 3.4%, from 3.5%. On paper this is a quiet, cooling report.

Two things argue against that. June undershot its consensus by the widest margin in a year, which means the forecasting models were already mis-specified going in. And the energy pass-through that everyone has been waiting for still has not appeared in the data — petrol was 26.7% higher year-on-year in June while core barely moved.

Producer prices follow on Thursday, expected at 4.9% year-on-year after 5.5%. If both come in soft, the September argument is effectively over before the Committee meets. If either runs hot, the 62% above goes higher.

WatchThe shelter component rather than the headline. If it decelerates again the street is right and the energy pass-through is being absorbed elsewhere.

03 · Energy — the reopening that stopped

A demand for compensation turned a reopening story into a stalemate.

Brent is at $87.75, up roughly 5% on Monday alone and higher for four straight sessions. It is more than 12% above last week's low, when the market was pricing an early settlement and a reopening of the Strait of Hormuz.

What changed was not military. Washington added a demand that Iran pay compensation as part of any agreement, and traders read it as removing the near-term path to a deal. Normal shipping through Hormuz now looks unlikely inside this month.

The distinction matters for how the price behaves from here. A reopening story is binary — it resolves and the premium drains in days. A stalemate accrues: every week without agreement adds a little more, and the price drifts up on no news at all. Oil and gas stocks led the market higher today while the index itself fell.

WatchHormuz transit counts rather than headlines. Traffic returning toward its pre-conflict average is the only confirmation that a settlement is real.

Sources: ICE Brent, 10–11 Aug 2026 · market commentary on settlement terms

04 · Markets — the long end

The bond market has already decided, and it did not wait for the data.

The ten-year yield sits at 4.71%, up six basis points overnight, after government bonds sold off across Asia-Pacific before New York opened. The dollar held firm and gold broke higher at the same time — a combination that reads as inflation anxiety rather than growth anxiety.

This matters because the long end answers to a different question than the policy rate does. The front end is waiting for tomorrow's CPI to tell it what the Committee will do in September. The long end is pricing what inflation looks like years out, and four consecutive days of rising crude has been enough to move it without any new data at all.

The practical edge of this is already visible. Mortgage rates are near a one-year high, and they follow the ten-year rather than the Fed. Equity valuations sit at their most stretched in decades against exactly this backdrop, which is why the index slipped today while oil and gas stocks led.

WatchWhether the ten-year and the two-year move together after tomorrow's print. If the long end keeps rising while the front end falls, the market is telling you it does not believe inflation is contained.

Sources: US Treasury daily yield curve, 10–11 Aug 2026 · Asia-Pacific bond market reports
