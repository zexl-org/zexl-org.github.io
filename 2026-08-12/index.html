Read the case behind each question

Four questions above, four short pieces below. Each explains why two sets of informed people are looking at the same facts and reaching different answers — and what would settle it.

01 · Prices — the Fed in September

A voting member spent Tuesday arguing for the opposite of what Wednesday's data suggests.

Core inflation came in at 2.5%, headline at 3.4%, both a tenth below June and both exactly on consensus. Nothing in that forces a central bank to act, and the market moved accordingly — September odds slipped roughly eight points.

The day before, Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack wrote publicly that now is the time to act, arguing that the longer the delay the more expensive the return to 2% becomes. She was one of three who dissented for an immediate rise on 29 July. Against that, Truist's head of US economics read the same release as supporting a hold.

This is the disagreement in its purest form: identical data, opposite readings, and a decision five weeks out. The tie-breaker is not this print but the next one — August CPI lands on 11 September, five days before the meeting.

02 · Prices — tomorrow at the factory gate

Producer prices have run two points above consumer prices all year. One of them is wrong.

Tomorrow's producer price index is expected at 4.9% year-on-year, down from 5.5%. Even at that level it sits well above the 3.4% consumers are paying, and that gap has persisted through the whole energy episode.

A gap like this resolves in one of two ways. Either firms absorb the difference in margins, which shows up later as weaker earnings, or they pass it through, which shows up later as consumer inflation. The second is what the Cleveland Fed nowcast has been waiting for since spring, and it has not arrived.

What makes tomorrow worth watching is that energy is still 14.7% higher than a year ago and gasoline 24.6%. Those are input costs. If producer inflation cools as fast as consumer inflation just did, the pass-through argument weakens considerably.

WatchServices PPI rather than goods. Goods track oil directly; services are where a genuine pass-through would show up first.

Sources: BLS Producer Price Index schedule · economist estimates, Aug 2026

03 · Growth — the number under the number

Prices have outrun pay for four straight months, and the gap widened again this morning.

Average hourly earnings are growing at 3.2% year-on-year. Consumer prices are rising at 3.4%. The difference is small on paper and total in practice: for four months, the average worker's pay has bought less than it did the year before.

This morning's release made it slightly worse. Earnings slipped 0.2% from a year earlier even as the inflation rate fell. Cooling prices and cooling wages arriving together is not relief — it is the same squeeze at a lower temperature.

It also explains why the central bank's position is harder than the headline suggests. A committee that raises rates into falling real wages tightens on households that are already losing ground. A committee that waits risks the gap staying negative for a fifth month, then a sixth.

04 · Energy — what the cooling rests on

The energy index fell 1.5% this month. It is still 14.7% up on the year.

The reason July's inflation looked tame is visible in one line of the release: energy fell 1.5% on the month. Strip that out and the picture is less comfortable — gasoline is 24.6% more expensive than a year ago, and shelter still accounted for two-thirds of what rise there was.

So the cooling everyone is describing rests on crude staying roughly where it is. Brent held near $87.75 today after four sessions higher, and Washington's demand for compensation from Iran still blocks the reopening that would drain the risk premium.

The asymmetry is what matters for the question above. A settlement takes prices back toward $78 quickly. A stalemate does not spike them — it just adds a little premium each week, which is exactly the shape that keeps inflation elevated without ever producing a headline.
