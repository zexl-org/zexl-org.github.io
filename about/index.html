What this is

Excellent judgment about the world is not concentrated where the credentials are.

Every morning, ZEXL puts four questions in front of you. Each one is a place where informed people are publicly disagreeing — traders against forecasters, one official body against another — and each one has a date on which somebody turns out to be wrong.

You give a probability. It takes about forty seconds. When the date arrives, the answer is compared against what actually happened, and your answer is compared against everyone else's.

That is the entire product. Underneath each question sits a short brief explaining why the disagreement exists, drawn from primary documents — central bank statements, official releases, regulatory filings — rather than from other people's coverage of them.

Why it works this way

Commentators make confident claims every day and are never graded. Institutions have credentials but no scorecard. Prediction markets have money at stake but no reasoning attached. Forums have reasoning but no measurement.

A measured, open, permanent record of human judgment did not exist. That gap is what this is for.

In two years, someone reading this will be quoted by name on monetary policy — with no PhD and no institution behind them.

They will have a record instead: two hundred questions answered, scored against what actually happened, better than the room.

There is no application. There is only the board, every morning, and what you do with it.

This is not wishful thinking. In 2014, a team of ordinary volunteers in a US intelligence research tournament out-forecast professional analysts who had access to classified material. Nobody knew those volunteers were any good — until someone kept score.

What you get, and when

  1. Immediately — where you sit against the professional reference points on today's questions.
  2. Within days — the first resolutions. Short-horizon questions are deliberately included so the loop closes fast.
  3. After about fifty resolved questions — a picture of your own judgment: which subjects you read well, and where you are consistently overconfident.
  4. After that — a public record. The readers with the strongest one get named here, with the numbers behind them.

Rules we hold ourselves to

Same time, daily
Published Monday to Thursday at the same hour. A daily record that skips days is not a record.
Nothing deleted
Every answer is kept with its timestamp, including ours, including the wrong ones. Revisions are kept too.
We get graded first
Every brief names something to watch and a date. When that date arrives, the result is published whether or not we called it right.
Method in the open
The index is rules-based. Components, weights and every revision are published, and when the method changes the whole series is recalculated.
No scoring fee, ever
Reading and answering are free permanently. If anything is ever paid, it will be access to your own history and the archive — never the ability to take part.
Not advice
Nothing here is investment, legal or policy advice, and no question is ever about casualties or any private individual.

Where it goes

As the number of answers grows, the aggregate becomes something that does not currently exist anywhere: a daily reading of where informed people disagree about the world, weighted by who has been right before.

Consensus forecasts are everywhere. A measure of disagreement, published daily and weighted by track record, is not. That is the number this is being built toward.
