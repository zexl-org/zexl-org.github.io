Today's story

Three central banks have turned. None agrees how far to go.

Five months ago the world was cutting rates. It is not any more — and the reason is not growth. It is a war in its fifth month that has put Brent above $100 and written itself into the official language of every major central bank this quarter.

What is new is not the turn. It is the disagreement inside it. The Fed held yesterday, but three regional presidents broke ranks and voted to raise. The Bank of Japan has three dissenters of its own on record. The ECB has already moved, into a growth forecast it cut at the same meeting.

None of these committees is arguing about direction. They are arguing about speed — which is the argument that moves the long end of every curve. One of the three answers tomorrow morning.

The five signals in this story
Fed holds a fifth time, but three presidents vote to raise

What happenedThe FOMC kept its range at 3.50–3.75% on a 9–3 vote. Hammack, Kashkari and Logan dissented in favour of an immediate quarter-point increase.

Why nowThe statement names the Middle East conflict as a source of elevated uncertainty and points to supply shocks driving prices in specific sectors, energy among them. Inflation has run above the 2% target for more than five years. Under Chair Warsh the statement has been cut back sharply and forward guidance withdrawn, so the vote split is now the clearest signal the Committee gives.

What's at stakeMarkets are pricing two hikes this year; the June projections showed one. If the split widens in September, the repricing runs through the long end — the 30-year already moved nine basis points on the day.

Bank of Japan decides tomorrow with three members already dissenting

What happenedThe Policy Board meets 30–31 July, with the decision and quarterly Outlook Report due Friday. June's meeting lifted the overnight call rate to 1.0% on a 7–1 vote — the first time at that level in three decades.

Why nowThe June statement committed to continuing to raise the policy rate while explicitly monitoring how the Middle East situation feeds into Japanese activity and prices. Deputy Governor Himino said in late June that wholesale inflation is accelerating as firms pass on conflict-driven costs, and that underlying inflation could overshoot the 2% target.

What's at stakeJapan is the last major economy with deeply negative real rates. A move tomorrow confirms the tightening turn is global rather than American, and the yen carries the adjustment either way.

ECB puts 2026 inflation at 3.0% — and cut growth at the same meeting

What happenedThe Governing Council lifted its three key rates by a quarter point in June. Staff projections show headline inflation averaging 3.0% this year, 2.3% in 2027 and 2.0% in 2028, with 2026 growth revised down to 0.8%.

Why nowBoth revisions trace to a higher energy price path. The Council called the decision robust across a range of scenarios for how the shock might evolve — the language of a bank raising into weakening growth deliberately.

What's at stakeUpside risk to inflation and downside risk to growth at once is the configuration with no comfortable answer. Europe is furthest along this path of the three.

Brent above $100, from $71 at the start of the month

What happenedBrent settled at $100.69 on 23 July, about 7% higher on the day and a fifth straight session of gains. WTI settled at $92.19. Brent opened the month at $71.57.

Why nowHouthi forces threatened to blockade Bab al-Mandeb, the main bypass for Hormuz. With both routes under threat at once, the market stopped pricing an alternative.

What's at stakeA 40% move in a month is the mechanism connecting every geopolitical item below to every monetary one above. The IEA put spare crude capacity near 4.4 million barrels a day, but more than three-quarters of it sits inside the war zone.

US strikes IRGC targets after Iranian missiles hit bases in Jordan

What happenedUS forces hit dozens of Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps sites overnight in retaliation for strikes on American bases in Jordan, which Amman says it intercepted. Washington also designated ten entities and eight tankers.

Why nowThe war began with US and Israeli strikes on 28 February. A June memorandum of understanding was meant to close it within sixty days, but fighting resumed in July after Iran struck three commercial vessels that had bypassed its approved routing.

What's at stakeThe exchange has now drawn in Jordan, Iraq, Saudi Arabia and Egypt. Each new participant lengthens the list of exposed energy assets and shipping lanes — the channel that reaches everything above.
