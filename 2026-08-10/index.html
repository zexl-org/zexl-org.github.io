Today's Brief

The jobs number moved the Fed. The inflation number, in two days, moves it back or seals it.

Read the case behind each question

Four questions above, four short pieces below. Each one explains why two sets of informed people are looking at the same facts and reaching different answers — and what would settle it.

01 · Prices — the Fed in September

Traders moved 14 points in a session. Forecasters have not moved at all.

Friday's employment report did something unusual: July payrolls declined outright and average hourly earnings rose less than expected. Bond yields fell, the ten-year settling three basis points lower at 4.65%, and the market's odds of a September rate rise dropped from 58% to 44% in a single session.

That is the labour half of the Fed's mandate easing. The price half has not. Energy costs from a war now in its sixth month are still working through the economy, and on 29 July three regional presidents — Hammack, Kashkari and Logan — dissented in favour of an immediate quarter-point rise while the Committee held at 3.50–3.75%.

So futures sit at 44% and the published economist consensus sits at effectively zero. The disagreement is not about the data; both groups have the same data. It is about which half of the mandate a stripped-down Committee weights when the two halves point in opposite directions.

WatchWhether any of the three dissenters speaks publicly before 16 September. Under this chair the vote split is the clearest guidance the Committee gives.

02 · Prices — Wednesday's inflation print

June missed low by the widest margin in a year. Both sides drew opposite lessons.

Core inflation came in at 2.6% against a 2.9% consensus in June, with the headline rate at 3.5%. Energy explains most of the gap between the two forecasts now on the table. It fell 5.7% month-on-month in June but was still 15.7% higher than a year earlier, with petrol alone up 26.7%.

A nowcast model that tracks fuel prices daily reads that as pass-through still arriving, and puts the number higher. The street's consensus assumes shelter costs keep cooling fast enough to absorb it, and puts the number lower. Both cannot be right at 8:30 on Wednesday morning.

This is the single largest input into the question above. A hot print and the 44% moves up sharply; a second soft one and the September argument is effectively over before the Committee meets.

WatchThe shelter component specifically, not the headline. If it decelerates again the street is right and the energy pass-through is being absorbed.

Sources: BLS Consumer Price Index, June 2026 · release schedule, bls.gov

03 · Growth — the payroll shock

Jobs fell. Unemployment did not. One of the two is wrong.

The unemployment rate has held at 4.2% through the summer. In July, the payroll count declined outright. These two series are measured from different surveys — one of employers, one of households — and they can drift apart for a month or two. They cannot describe opposite labour markets for a quarter.

The reconciliation usually runs one of two ways. Either the payroll fall was noise and August rebounds, or the household survey catches up and the unemployment rate starts climbing — which is the sequence that ends rate-rise conversations entirely.

What makes this the story rather than a data footnote is the timing. It landed three days before an inflation print and five weeks before a meeting the market prices as a coin flip. A central bank facing rising prices and falling employment at the same time has no comfortable option, and it is exactly the position the ECB has already been in since June.

WatchWeekly jobless claims each Thursday between now and 4 September. If claims rise for three consecutive weeks, the household survey is about to move.

Sources: US employment report, Aug 2026 · Department of Labor weekly claims

04 · Markets — the calm

The strange number today is not the Fed's. It is the one that has not moved.

The VIX closed at 14.90 on Friday and traded near 15.5 this morning — a normal, risk-on level. The S&P 500 sits at 7,757 and the Nasdaq at 26,690, both close to records. On its own that is unremarkable.

What it sits next to is not. An inflation print lands in two days. A Fed meeting five weeks out is priced as a coin flip by the same market. A war that began on 28 February has now failed to hold three separate ceasefires, and the energy agency's working assumption is that Gulf shipping does not return to pre-conflict levels until early 2027.

Calm markets are usually right, which is why they are calm. The asymmetry is what matters: the index cannot fall much below 12 without signalling outright complacency, but it can reach 30 or 40 on a single bad print. It went above 31 in March on the oil spike.

WatchWhether equity and Treasury volatility diverge. Persistent gaps between the two have preceded regime shifts more reliably than either on its own.

Sources: Cboe VIX, 7–10 Aug 2026 · EIA Short-Term Energy Outlook
